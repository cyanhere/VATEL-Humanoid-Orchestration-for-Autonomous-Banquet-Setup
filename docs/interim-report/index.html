1. Introduction

1.1. Problem of Interest

Banquet table setting requires handling diverse, fragile tableware across an environment purpose-built for human reach and access, where fixed automation is impractical and a separate task-specific robot per item type is uneconomical. This project develops and evaluates an autonomous perception-and-manipulation pipeline for a humanoid robot to transfer and place banquet tableware, using plate transfer from an AGV (a requirement from our industry partner, who is deploying the AGV) as the representative case, with the approach intended to generalise to other tableware handling tasks.

Back the statement with evidence: why setting tables is labour-intensive, why tableware is hard to handle (fragile, varied), and why a hall built for people rules out fixed automation. Use the Five Whys to show you are treating the cause, not a symptom. (Rubric A1) ≈ 150 words

1.4. Objectives and Scope

The project's objectives are:

  1. A planner app, used from an iPad or a computer, in which the banquet team sees the whole hall with its tables segmented on a 2-D map, plans the arrangement, and sends the plan to the robot.
  2. Autonomous navigation of the hall by the Unitree R1 from the planner's static map, with the onboard Hesai JT128 lidar correcting the table positions so the robot can reach every set position.
  3. Autonomous transfer of plates, one at a time, from a stack on an AGV to their designated positions.
  4. Evaluation of the complete pipeline against the success criteria in Section 3.2.

4.4. Selected Concept and System Architecture

VATEL runs in five steps:

  1. Plan. The banquet team uses the VATEL Planner on an iPad or a computer. It shows the whole hall, segments out the tables and draws them on a 2-D map, where the team plans the arrangement and setup for the event.
  2. Hand off. The planner sends the static map to the R1: where each table is, how many sets of plates and cutlery each table needs, and the calculated position of each set.
  3. Navigate. The R1 navigates the hall from that static map. Its JT128 lidar maps the real room to find the exact location of each table, correcting the difference between the plan and the real arrangement, and the map is updated so the robot can reach every set position.
  4. Transfer. Once the robot reaches at least 80 % of the required workspace, it goes to the AGV plate-delivery area and takes one plate at a time off the stack, placing each at its designated position.
  5. Done. At least 90 % of the plates are at their designated positions, and no plate is broken.

Why this concept follows from the evaluation in 4.3, and how the subsystems divide the work. Section 5 details each one. (A2) ≈ 150 words
